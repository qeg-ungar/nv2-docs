# System Overview

This section breaks down all of the hardware assemblies and components of the NV2 setup, as well as provides an overview of the software control modules for performing experiments. 

The system level layout for the setup is shown below. The majority of the instruments and components can be divided into two main groups: RF/control electronics and optics/imaging. In the system block diagram below I include a third group called "science box" which contains all of the hardware inside the temperature controlled optical enclosure where the diamond sample is mounted. This also includes the piezo and magnet stages, the objective lens, and the dichroic mirror plus filters.

At the highest level, the setup is designed to peform two main tasks: scanning fluorescence microscopy (imaging mode) and microwave pulse sequencing with optical readout (sequencing mode). We will dive deeper into these two modes in {doc}`../experiments/index`. For imaging and readout, we use green light to illumunate the NV centers in the diamond and image the red NV fluorescence on a single photon detector or camera. For confocal microscopy, we use the piezo stage to scan in three dimensions (first along Z to find the NV layer in the objective focus and then along X-Y to image the arrays or single emitters). The fluorescence is measured on a single photon detector (SPCM). For widefield imaging, the beam illuminates a larger field of view so we can see many NVs at once (we still use the piezo stage to center the wider beam to the correct position). The light within this field of view is imaged onto the SPAD camera. We record the SPCM output signal using the NI DAQ card interfaced with the PC. All of the controls for acquiring scanning images are configured through the Qudi software interface built in python. The SPAD camera is controlled directly by the PC over a USB 3.0 connection. Acquisition and image processing is also done through Qudi.

For experimental sequences, the microwave pulses generated by the Quantum Machine OPX+ are used to resonantly control the electronic spin state of the NV, which can then be measured through the fluorescence intensity with our detectors. The microwave signal is frequency mixed to the resonance frequency of the NV (between 2-4 GHz) and amplified to drive a MW stripline connected to the PCB, creating an AC magnetic field to couple to the electronic spin. The setup can also be configured to perform nuclear spin control (for nitrogen or carbon isotopes) with high power RF pulses up to 200 MHz. The fluorescence signal is recorded by the OPX+ during the readout pulse. All the software control for pulse sequencing and data processing of the readout signals are executed in a separate python environment which interfaces with the QM-OPX+. 

The typical experiment workflow consists of first performing a fluorescence scan to locate the spot of interest in the sample (perhaps a single NV or ensemble spot). A tracking step is then run to focus the position of the sample relative to the objective and to maximize the fluorescence from the NVs. We then move to the OPX+ code environment and run calibration experiments to tune the IQ microwave control frequency and amplitude for qubit control. Following calibration, we can then run more complex experimental sequences such as spin-echo, CPMG, DEER, etc. Since single NV measurements suffer from low SNR (we collect around .05 photons per readout pulse), it can take on the order of hours of averaging to achieve good SNR depening on the duration of the experimental sequence. Our control code for both the Qudi scanning and QM sequencing is fully automated and can be run autonomously while keeping the piezo position and IQ frequency optimized (see {doc}`../experiments/automation`). In addition, I have equipped the setup with a PID controlled heater to stabilize the temperature in the science box in order to minimize sample and magnet drift.
